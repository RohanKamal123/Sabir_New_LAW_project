01The legal world in five minutes

Before the product makes sense, you need the place it lives in. This is the short version.

Bangladesh has one Supreme Court with two divisions

A barrister of the Supreme Court of Bangladesh practises before two bodies that sit inside the same building:

  • The Appellate Division (the system calls it Division 1) — the final court of appeal. It hears appeals from the High Court Division and, on "leave," decides whether to reopen a decided case.
  • The High Court Division (Division 2) — the first-tier Superior Court. It hears original matters (like writ petitions against the government) and appeals from the lower district courts.

Cases are not heard by the whole court. A handful of judges sit together as a bench — typically two judges for a High Court Division bench, and three or more for an Appellate Division bench. A bench sits in a specific courtroom on a specific day.

How a barrister's day actually runs

  1. The evening before a hearing, the Court publishes the cause list — the roster of every matter due before each bench the next day, with its serial number and what it is listed for (a hearing, a judgment, a date to be fixed).
  2. The barrister must find their matters in that list. They appear by their own name (as an "advocate" or "appearing counsel"), by the parties' names, or by the case number.
  3. In court, the bench does something to the case — hears argument, fixes a date, adjourns it, or delivers an order or judgment. That becomes an entry in the case history.
  4. Between appearances, the barrister drafts documents, watches deadlines (some set by statute, not by the judge), and keeps files for every client.

The four repetitive, easy-to-miss tasks above — check the list, track the case, don't miss the deadline, draft correctly — are exactly what this product automates.

02Plain-English glossary

Every legal term the product surfaces, explained the way you'd explain it to a friend. Skim this once; the feature section will then read easily.

TermWhat it means
Cause listThe Court's daily roster. A long list, printed in a specific layout, showing every matter listed before each bench on a given day, with a serial number and a reason ("For Hearing," "For Judgment," "Fixing a date of hearing"). Published the evening before. This is the document the product's design is based on.
BenchThe group of judges who sit together to hear a case on a given day.
SerialA matter's position on a cause list. Which serial matters enormously — serial 3 "For Judgment" is a very different moment than serial 3 "For Hearing."
AdvocateA lawyer who appears and argues before the court. In a cause list, the barrister's own name appears in the advocates' column.
PartyOne of the people or organisations on a side of a case — e.g. a client.
Petitioner / RespondentIn a writ or petition, the petitioner is the one who filed it (asking the court to act); the respondent is the side asked to respond.
Appellant / Respondent (appeal)In an appeal, the appellant is the side who lost below and is appealing; the respondent is the side defending the earlier decision.
Case numberThe court's identifier for a matter, e.g. First Appeal 226/2013 or Civil Rule 832(FM)/2006. Not a simple integer — "832(FM)" is real. The product treats it as text, not a number.
AdjournmentThe court postpones the matter to another day. Recorded in the case history.
JudgmentThe court's reasoned decision. A decree is the formal, enforceable outcome that follows a judgment.
Statute / Act / SectionA written law passed by Parliament. A statute divides into sections (s. 5, s. 12…). The Bangladesh Code is the official collection of these acts.
Limitation periodA law-set time limit within which a legal step (e.g. filing an appeal) must be taken. File too late and the court can refuse to hear you at all — the matter is barred. The Limitation Act 1908 sets these periods.
Cause of actionThe set of facts giving someone the right to sue or appeal. The limitation clock often starts when the cause of action arises (e.g. the date of the order you want to challenge).
Condonation of delayCourt permission to file late, if the delay is excusable. Section 5 of the Limitation Act lets a court admit a late filing if sufficient cause is shown — which is why the calculator warns "s. 5 may still permit admission" when a period has expired.
Writ petitionA petition to the High Court Division seeking a remedy — commonly against the government (challenging an order, an acquisition, or a breach of rights).
Leave to appealPermission from the Appellate Division to hear an appeal. Getting "leave" is a stage in itself.
Rule NisiA common writ remedy: the court issues a rule calling on respondents to show cause why the court should not act. The standard prayer in a writ petition.
GroundsThe numbered legal reasons ("That…") a petition or appeal should succeed.
PrayersThe specific orders the petitioner asks the court to make at the end of a petition.
AffidavitA sworn written statement of facts. A "reply affidavit" answers an opponent's affidavit.
Copy time (s. 12)When counting a limitation period, the law can exclude time spent obtaining a copy of the decree/order (s. 12 of the Limitation Act). The calculator shows this exclusion in its working.

03Feature by feature

For each screen: what the lawyer sees, what it legally means, and why it matters to them.

F1 Cause-list watch & alerts

Today screen · cause-list screen · Telegram

The barrister tells the product their own name, their cases, and their clients' names. Every evening the product reads the Court's published cause list, finds every matter that matches, and sends one Telegram message listing them all.

What the lawyer sees"Cause list 24/08/2026: 2 matter(s) listed — First Appeal 226/2013, High Court Division, Court No. 18, Serial 10 — Fixing a date of hearing. Matched: case listed."
What it legally meansThe matter is on tomorrow's roster. "For Hearing" vs "For Judgment" vs "Fixing a date" tells the barrister what will happen — whether to prepare argument, expect a decision, or just take the next date.
Why it mattersA missed hearing is a default, a wasted day, or a disservice to a client who travelled. The barrister would otherwise scan ~65 pages at 9pm looking for their own name.
The clever bitConnected matters heard "(with)" another carry their parent's serial; "832(FM)" is not an integer; the barrister's own files get a margin rule, like a pen mark on paper.

F2 Case-status tracker

Case status screen · case search

The product tracks a case's full hearing history from the Court's own case-search pages, and only flags the changes that matter — a new listing, a result filled in, or a result amended.

What the lawyer sees"Adjourned till 07.09.2026" appears as a new result where there was none before.
What it legally meansThe case history is the official record of every order, adjournment and judgment. A change here is the court actually deciding something about the case.
Why it mattersOld matters move slowly. A result can appear on a case the barrister had stopped watching — the tracker notices the difference so no one has to re-check 50 pages by hand.
The clever bitIt reports three things only (new listing, result filled, result amended) — not the whole history every time, which would bury the signal in noise.

F3 Statute lookup

Statutes screen

Search the official Bangladesh Code (e.g. bdlaws.minlaw.gov.bd) for the exact text of a section. Results are verbatim — never summarised, never generated.

What the lawyer seesSection 5 of the Limitation Act, word for word, with the source URL attached.
What it legally meansThe exact statutory wording is what a judge will read; quoting it precisely is what wins. A statute like the Limitation Act governs rights and time limits across all civil practice.
Why it mattersKnowing a section exists is not the same as having its exact text at hand mid-research. This removes the "which act, which section, which words?" guesswork.
The clever bitZero hallucination risk by design — it returns what the official Code says, not what a model remembers. That is the whole point of exact-text lookup.

F4 Template-based drafting

Drafting screen

The barrister picks a document type (writ petition, leave to appeal, stay application, condonation application), supplies parties, facts, grounds and prayers, and the product assembles the document. The structure comes from a fixed template; an AI model writes only the narrative from the facts given.

What the lawyer sees"IN THE SUPREME COURT OF BANGLADESH…" — a structured petition with the facts laid into the right places, ready to be read, corrected and settled.
What it legally meansA Supreme Court petition has a fixed conventional shape (parties, subject, facts, grounds, prayers). Getting that shape right is non-negotiable; the legal reasoning is the human's job.
Why it mattersDrafting is slow and error-prone. A solid first skeleton cut from the barrister's own facts saves hours — but the barrister must verify every date, figure and authority before filing.
The clever bitThe citation guard: the AI is forbidden from citing any authority the barrister did not supply, and anything it slips in is flagged. No invented case references.

F5 Limitation calculator

Limitation screen

The barrister enters a start date and the kind of legal step (e.g. an appeal), and the product computes the filing deadline under the Limitation Act — showing its working, rule by rule.

What the lawyer seesA date, with steps: "s. 12(2) — exclude 15 days for the copy of the decree… add back 15 days → due 2026-04-30," plus the source citations.
What it legally meansDeadlines are set by law, not by the judge. Sections 4 (court closed), 5 (condonation), and 12 (copy time) adjust a raw number of days into the true filing date.
Why it mattersA wrong deadline can bar the client's cause of action entirely — the single most expensive mistake in this product. A figure you cannot audit is a figure you cannot file on.
The clever bitIt refuses to answer from any limitation article a lawyer has not verified. Machine-extracted from a PDF, every article stays "unverified" until a human checks it — better to say "I don't know" than to guess.

F6 Matter / case-file management

Matters screen · one matter · diary

A lightweight case file per client: the client's details, every case in the file, notes, documents, time entries and deadlines — all in one place, linked to the case so the watch covers it automatically.

What the lawyer sees"Ruhul Amin v Aiyub Ali — First Appeal 226/2013 [MAT-2026-001], 150 mins @ Tk 6000/hr, diary: reply affidavit overdue."
What it legally meansA "matter" is a client's entire dispute, which can span several cases, hearings and documents over years. The diary holds every deadline that could be missed.
Why it mattersThis is the daily-habit layer: it turns a set of single-use tools into the barrister's desk. Time logged is time billed; a deadline caught is a client's case saved.
The clever bitOpening a file with a case number also starts watching that case — so the file and the cause-list alert are the same thread, not two systems.

F7 Telegram bot

Telegram

The same features reachable from a messaging app — today's listings, the diary, case and statute lookups, quick notes and time entries.

What the lawyer sees/today, /diary, /case First Appeal 226 2013, /statute s. 5 of the Limitation Act, /note MAT-2026-001 …
What it legally meansThe legal work is the same; only the surface changes. The point is that it sits in an app the barrister already has open, next to their clients.
Why it mattersA tool embedded in an already-open surface gets used; a separate dashboard gets ignored. This is how the alerts actually reach the barrister at 9pm.
The clever bitOne set of services behind every surface — the bot, the web UI, the CLI and the API can never disagree with each other.

05Where the product deliberately says "no"

The parts that look like missing features are the product being careful — and that is exactly what a barrister should trust.

No invented case lawIt never answers with a Bangladeshi judgment from memory. Case-law reporters are paywalled, and even the big global tools invent citations 17–33% of the time. This product refuses rather than risk a false citation.
No unverified limitation articlesThe First Schedule was machine-extracted from a PDF. Until a lawyer checks each article, the calculator refuses to compute from it. An unverified figure could bar a client's case — so it waits.
No generated statute textStatute results are verbatim from the official Bangladesh Code, with the source attached. Exact text or nothing.
No drafting without the barrister's citationsThe drafting AI may only cite authorities the barrister supplied. Anything else is flagged as unverified.

In every case, the rule is the same: a place where this software could be confidently wrong is marked, or it refuses to answer at all. That honesty is the product's core design principle — and the reason it is practice software, not a legal oracle.
