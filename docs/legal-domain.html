01The legal world in five minutes

Before the product makes sense, you need the place it lives in. This is the short version.

Why this tool, this time. For decades a barrister's evening ritual was unchanged: wait for the next morning's cause list to be circulated, then scan page after page by hand for a name. That has quietly changed. The Supreme Court now publishes its cause lists — and its case-status records — directly on supremecourt.gov.bd as plain pages and PDFs, issued the evening before a hearing, by division and by bench. There is no official API, but the material is now machine-readable at the exact moment it matters. That window is the whole reason this product exists: it reads the list the evening it drops, so a barrister no longer has to. Paper files, email notices and a desk diary are still the working tools of the trade — this tool does not replace them, it sits beside them and does the scanning.

Bangladesh has one Supreme Court with two divisions

A barrister of the Supreme Court of Bangladesh practises before two bodies that sit inside the same building:

  • The Appellate Division (the system calls it Division 1) — the final court of appeal. It hears appeals from the High Court Division and, on "leave," decides whether to reopen a decided case.
  • The High Court Division (Division 2) — the first-tier Superior Court. It hears original matters (like writ petitions against the government) and appeals from the lower district courts.

Cases are not heard by the whole court. A handful of judges sit together as a bench — typically two judges for a High Court Division bench, and three or more for an Appellate Division bench. A bench sits in a specific courtroom on a specific day.

How a barrister's day actually runs

  1. The evening before a hearing, the Court publishes the cause list — the roster of every matter due before each bench the next day, with its serial number and what it is listed for (a hearing, a judgment, a date to be fixed).
  2. The barrister must find their matters in that list. They appear by their own name (as an "advocate" or "appearing counsel"), by the parties' names, or by the case number.
  3. In court, the bench does something to the case — hears argument, fixes a date, adjourns it, or delivers an order or judgment. That becomes an entry in the case history.
  4. Between appearances, the barrister drafts documents, watches deadlines (some set by statute, not by the judge), and keeps files for every client.

This is the form the barrister is fighting with, every evening:

The oxblood margin rule on serial 10 marks the barrister's own matter — the same way he'd mark his printed list with a pen; it is his name in the advocates' column that earns the alert. Serial 3 "For Judgment" is a very different moment than serial 3 "For Hearing".

The four repetitive, easy-to-miss tasks above — check the list, track the case, don't miss the deadline, draft correctly — are exactly what this product automates.

Common situations barristers face

Real practice does not line up in neat rows, and the product is built for the mess:

  • A case listed in both divisions at once. An appeal travels: a Writ Petition or Civil Revision in the High Court Division can be challenged further in the Appellate Division. Same parties, two case numbers, two benches, two cause lists. The barrister needs both watched — and they are, because one file can hold several cases, each with its own watch.
  • Petitioner and respondent swap roles. A man who is petitioner in the writ becomes respondent in the appeal the other side files. The role changes; the dispute does not. It is still one matter, whatever the current heading says.
  • One matter, several cases. A dispute rarely lives in a single proceeding: a suit below, a First Appeal against the decree, a Civil Revision after that. All sit inside one file.
  • Connected and adjourned matters. A matter "(with Serial 10)" is heard alongside its parent, not under its own listing. Missing the parent serial is the same as missing the child — the product carries the parent's serial through so one watch covers both.

02Plain-English glossary

Every legal term the product surfaces, explained the way you'd explain it to a friend. Skim this once; the feature section will then read easily.

TermWhat it means
Cause listThe Court's daily roster. A long list, printed in a specific layout, showing every matter listed before each bench on a given day, with a serial number and a reason ("For Hearing," "For Judgment," "Fixing a date of hearing"). Published the evening before. This is the document the product's design is based on.
BenchThe group of judges who sit together to hear a case on a given day.
SerialA matter's position on a cause list. Which serial matters enormously — serial 3 "For Judgment" is a very different moment than serial 3 "For Hearing."
AdvocateA lawyer who appears and argues before the court. In a cause list, the barrister's own name appears in the advocates' column.
PartyOne of the people or organisations on a side of a case — e.g. a client.
Petitioner / RespondentIn a writ or petition, the petitioner is the one who filed it (asking the court to act); the respondent is the side asked to respond.
Appellant / Respondent (appeal)In an appeal, the appellant is the side who lost below and is appealing; the respondent is the side defending the earlier decision.
Case numberThe court's identifier for a matter, e.g. First Appeal 226/2013 or Civil Rule 832(FM)/2006. Not a simple integer — "832(FM)" is real. The product treats it as text, not a number.
CaseOne specific proceeding in a court, identified by its case number and the division it sits in. A case has its own history, its own list entry and its own bench. The court thinks in cases.
MatterThe client's entire dispute — the human story that may span several cases, hearings, documents and years. The product builds its file around the matter, not around any single case.
FileThe barrister's own record-keeping folder for a matter: one client, one or more cases, plus the notes, documents, time entries and deadlines that accrete around them. Carries a chamber-style reference like MAT-2026-001. A matter and its file are the same object seen from two sides — the dispute, and the papers that follow it.
Case historyThe official record of what a court has actually done to one case — every order, adjournment and judgment, in order. Not the same as a matter history: each case in a matter has its own history.
AdjournmentThe court postpones the matter to another day. Recorded in the case history.
JudgmentThe court's reasoned decision. A decree is the formal, enforceable outcome that follows a judgment.
Statute / Act / SectionA written law passed by Parliament. A statute divides into sections (s. 5, s. 12…). The Bangladesh Code is the official collection of these acts.
Limitation periodA law-set time limit within which a legal step (e.g. filing an appeal) must be taken. File too late and the court can refuse to hear you at all — the matter is barred. The Limitation Act 1908 sets these periods.
Cause of actionThe set of facts giving someone the right to sue or appeal. The limitation clock often starts when the cause of action arises (e.g. the date of the order you want to challenge).
Condonation of delayCourt permission to file late, if the delay is excusable. Section 5 of the Limitation Act lets a court admit a late filing if sufficient cause is shown — which is why the calculator warns "s. 5 may still permit admission" when a period has expired.
Writ petitionA petition to the High Court Division seeking a remedy — commonly against the government (challenging an order, an acquisition, or a breach of rights).
Leave to appealPermission from the Appellate Division to hear an appeal. Getting "leave" is a stage in itself.
Rule NisiA common writ remedy: the court issues a rule calling on respondents to show cause why the court should not act. The standard prayer in a writ petition.
GroundsThe numbered legal reasons ("That…") a petition or appeal should succeed.
PrayersThe specific orders the petitioner asks the court to make at the end of a petition.
AffidavitA sworn written statement of facts. A "reply affidavit" answers an opponent's affidavit.
Copy time (s. 12)When counting a limitation period, the law can exclude time spent obtaining a copy of the decree/order (s. 12 of the Limitation Act). The calculator shows this exclusion in its working.

How the four fit together

One matter, the client's whole dispute; several cases under it; one file that holds them:

MATTER — "Ruhul Amin v Aiyub Ali" (the dispute)
  ├─ CASE 1 — First Appeal 226/2013 (current hearing, High Court Division)
  ├─ CASE 2 — Writ Petition 3421/2025 (related challenge)
  └─ FILE — MAT-2026-001 (the barrister's folder for the matter)
Each case carries its own case number and its own case history; the file carries everything the barrister adds by hand — notes, documents, time, deadlines.

03Feature by feature

For each screen: the question a barrister actually asks, and what the product does about it.

How it works with what you already have

This product is a companion to a paper practice, not a replacement for it. The web UI prints correctly, so the bench list taken to court can stand in for the Court's own page; the oxblood margin rule on your own matters mirrors the pen mark you already make. Court notices still arrive by email — the alerts sit in Telegram, an app already open on your phone, next to your clients. Your existing filing system and diary become the file and the diary views; nothing kept on paper is discarded, only indexed.

Scope: what this covers, and what it does not

It covers: the two divisions of the Supreme Court of Bangladesh — the Appellate Division and the High Court Division — for cause-list watch, case-status tracking, statute lookup and limitation work. A matter can hold cases in both divisions at once, and the product handles that.

It does not cover: the district and subordinate courts, e-filing, court-system integration, or case-law research. Those are outside its scope by design — see where the product deliberately says "no".

F1 Cause-list watch & alerts

Today screen · cause-list screen · Telegram

Is anything of mine in court tomorrow?

What you see

"Cause list 24/08/2026: 2 matter(s) listed — First Appeal 226/2013, High Court Division, Court No. 18, Serial 10 — Fixing a date of hearing. Matched: case listed."

What it means

The matter is on tomorrow's roster. "For Hearing" vs "For Judgment" vs "Fixing a date" tells the barrister what will happen — whether to prepare argument, expect a decision, or just take the next date.

Why it matters

A missed hearing is a default, a wasted day, or a disservice to a client who travelled. The barrister would otherwise scan ~65 pages at 9pm looking for their own name.

The clever bit

Connected matters heard "(with)" another carry their parent's serial; 832(FM) is not an integer; the barrister's own files get a margin rule, like a pen mark on paper.

F2 Case-status tracker

Case status screen · case search

Has anything changed on my cases?

What you see

"Adjourned till 07.09.2026" appears as a new result where there was none before.

What it means

The case history is the official record of every order, adjournment and judgment. A change here is the court actually deciding something about the case.

Why it matters

Old matters move slowly. A result can appear on a case the barrister had stopped watching — the tracker notices the difference so no one has to re-check 50 pages by hand.

The clever bit

It reports three things only (new listing, result filled, result amended) — not the whole history every time, which would bury the signal in noise.

F3 Statute lookup

Statutes screen

What exactly does s. 5 say?

What you see

Section 5 of the Limitation Act, word for word, with the source URL attached.

What it means

The exact statutory wording is what a judge will read; quoting it precisely is what wins. A statute like the Limitation Act governs rights and time limits across all civil practice.

Why it matters

Knowing a section exists is not the same as having its exact text at hand mid-research. This removes the "which act, which section, which words?" guesswork.

The clever bit

Zero hallucination risk by design — it returns what the official Code says, not what a model remembers. That is the whole point of exact-text lookup.

A note

Text is verbatim from the official Bangladesh Code (bdlaws.minlaw.gov.bd) and is current as of the day it was fetched. For amendments after that date, check the Code directly — the product never guesses at later changes.

F4 Template-based drafting

Drafting screen

Give me the skeleton of this writ petition.

What you see

"IN THE SUPREME COURT OF BANGLADESH…" — a structured petition with the facts laid into the right places, ready to be read, corrected and settled.

What it means

A Supreme Court petition has a fixed conventional shape (parties, subject, facts, grounds, prayers). Getting that shape right is non-negotiable; the legal reasoning is the human's job.

Why it matters

Drafting is slow and error-prone. A solid first skeleton cut from the barrister's own facts saves hours — but the barrister must verify every date, figure and authority before filing.

The clever bit

The citation guard: the AI is forbidden from citing any authority the barrister did not supply, and anything it slips in is flagged. No invented case references.

F5 Limitation calculator

Limitation screen

When is the last day I can file this appeal?

What you see

A date, with steps: "s. 12(2) — exclude 15 days for the copy of the decree… add back 15 days → due 2026-04-30," plus the source citations.

What it means

Deadlines are set by law, not by the judge. Sections 4 (court closed), 5 (condonation), and 12 (copy time) adjust a raw number of days into the true filing date.

Why it matters

A wrong deadline can bar the client's cause of action entirely — the single most expensive mistake in this product. A figure you cannot audit is a figure you cannot file on.

The clever bit

It refuses to answer from any limitation article a lawyer has not verified. Machine-extracted from a PDF, every article stays "unverified" until a human checks it — better to say "I don't know" than to guess.

F6 Matter / case-file management

Matters screen · one matter · diary

What is owed on this file, and what's overdue?

What you see

"Ruhul Amin v Aiyub Ali — First Appeal 226/2013 [MAT-2026-001], 150 mins @ Tk 6000/hr, diary: reply affidavit overdue."

Time & billing

Time is logged per entry — /time MAT-2026-001 90 "Settling grounds" — and the rate is stored on each entry, so it can differ from one client or matter to the next. The matter view rolls minutes into hours, shows what is billed and what is not, and a value. It is a ledger a barrister can bill from, not an invoicing engine.

What it means

A matter is a client's entire dispute, which can span several cases, hearings and documents over years (see the glossary frame). The file — MAT-2026-001 — is the barrister's folder for it. The diary holds every deadline that could be missed.

Why it matters

This is the daily-habit layer: it turns a set of single-use tools into the barrister's desk. Time logged is time billed; a deadline caught is a client's case saved.

The clever bit

Opening a file with a case number also starts watching that case — so the file and the cause-list alert are the same thread, not two systems.

04A barrister's week, retold in plain terms

The same journey the UI walkthrough shows — here with every legal term unpacked. Teal = the machine working while he sleeps; black = his own hands; the brass spine marks the hours he is actually in court.

And the week this product saves him from. Without it, each beat above is an evening and a morning lost:

  • A missed hearing. The list is 65 pages and his matter sits at serial 70, deep in the middle. Nobody calls. His client has travelled from outside Dhaka; the bench fixes the date, the matter is adjourned, and the client's day — and his fee — is gone.
  • A wrong deadline. He counts 90 days from the decree but forgets the copy time, or that Friday and Saturday are closed. The appeal is filed a day late and the court refuses to hear it at all; the cause of action is barred, and the loss is the client's, on his name.
  • An hour gone at 9pm. Reading the whole list line by line, hunting his own name, then the client's, then the case numbers. It is not research; it is scanning, and it has to be done again tomorrow.
  • A conflict nobody saw. Two files with deadlines on the same day, or a "For Hearing" and a "For Judgment" the same morning in different courts. Paper diaries do not shout at you; this product does.

05Where the product deliberately says "no"

The parts that look like missing features are the product being careful — and that is exactly what a barrister should trust.

No invented case law

It never answers with a Bangladeshi judgment from memory. Case-law reporters are paywalled, and even the big global tools invent citations 17–33% of the time. This product refuses rather than risk a false citation.

No unverified limitation articles

The First Schedule was machine-extracted from a PDF. Until a lawyer checks each article, the calculator refuses to compute from it. An unverified figure could bar a client's case — so it waits.

This is what that looks like in the product — an article the calculator will not touch, and the same article once a barrister has checked it:

Art. 152Thirty days — from the date of the decree or order appealed from▲ unverified
Art. 152Thirty days — from the date of the decree or order appealed fromverified

To move an article from the first line to the second: run barrister review-queue to see what still needs checking, open the official text at the source URL, and flip the article to verified in the schedule. The calculator only computes from verified articles, and the diary drops its "not verified by a lawyer" warning once a deadline's basis is checked.

No generated statute text

Statute results are verbatim from the official Bangladesh Code, with the source attached. Exact text or nothing.

No drafting without the barrister's citations

The drafting AI may only cite authorities the barrister supplied. Anything else is flagged as unverified.

In every case, the rule is the same: a place where this software could be confidently wrong is marked, or it refuses to answer at all. That honesty is the product's core design principle — and the reason it is practice software, not a legal oracle.
